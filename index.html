6.1 · History of Human Population Change

Population Predictions

10,000 years ago, only 2–3 million people lived on Earth, and population stayed stable for millennia — until agriculture changed everything, enabling rapid expansion.

  • Malthus (1798): warned that food production couldn't keep pace with population growth.
  • Thomas Vogt (1948): predicted humans had already met Earth's carrying capacity, and that 75% of humans would be wiped out once population reached 3 billion.
  • Paul Ehrlich (1968): predicted a population collapse by the 1980s.

6.1 · Historical Population Crashes

Population Crashes

Black Death (1347–1352)
Bubonic plague killed 30–60% of Europe's population.
Easter Island
Rapa Nui civilization collapsed after deforesting the island, degrading once-fertile land beyond recovery.
Ancestral Puebloans

Viking Greenland
Deforestation for livestock triggered topsoil erosion; a cold period drove surviving colonists away.

6.2 · What Has Driven Recent Population Growth?

TFR, Death Rates, and Carrying Capacity

0.2 ha
land to support each person today — less than half a football field
  • Total Fertility Rate (TFR): average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime. A TFR of 2.1 = replacement fertility (population neither grows nor declines).
  • Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Carrying capacity: the maximum population a habitat can sustainably support.
  • Population growth occurs when the birth rate exceeds the death rate.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
6.2 · How Much Human Activity Can Earth Support?

Carrying Capacity and Its Costs

Humans have raised Earth's carrying capacity for our species through technology — but those gains carry costs: water and air pollution, climate change, and biodiversity loss.

  • Carrying capacity is the maximum population an environment can support sustainably.
  • The key question is not how many people Earth can support, but at what level of environmental impact.

6.4 · How Is Global Population Changing?

The Demographic Transition

A decrease in birth and death rates linked to improvements in living conditions, birth control, and economic growth. Most countries are somewhere on this transition today.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
6.4 · The Three Phases of Demographic Transition

From High to Low Death and Birth Rates

1 · Mortality Transition
Death rates fall as food, water, and medical care improve. Birth rates stay high → rapid population growth.
2 · Fertility Transition
Societal changes reduce birth rates even as death rates stay low → growth slows. Demographic window: the workforce dominates.
3 · Stability Transition
Low birth rates match low death rates → zero population growth. Some countries now face below-replacement TFRs.

6.5 · US Demographic Transition — The Baby Boom

An American Case Study

2.1 → 3.7
TFR during the baby boom (1946–1964)
  • 1935–1955: the US death rate fell from 11 to 9.3 per 1,000; infant mortality dropped from 58 to 28 per 1,000.
  • TFR rose from 2.1 to 3.7 → the 'baby boom' generation, born 1946–1964.
  • Baby-boomer TFR fell back to 2.1 by the early 1970s — the fertility transition began.
  • By the time boomers reached retirement age (2011), the workforce had surpassed 75% of the population — the demographic window at work.
  • Question: What does it indicate if a population pyramid has a broad base?
  • Birth rates are high.

6.5 · What Factors Facilitate a Demographic Transition?

Urbanization, Health, and Education

Urbanization
People moving to cities for jobs have lower fertility. Global average urban TFR is 2.2 vs. 3.0 in rural areas.
Health Care
Falling infant mortality reduces the incentive to have many children 'to ensure some survive' — fewer but healthier children.
Education
Higher literate life expectancy (LLE) predicts lower TFR. Education, especially for girls, is the most powerful predictor of fertility decline.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
6.6 · How Does Empowering Women Change Populations?

Education, Opportunity, and Fertility

  • Population declines rapidly when large numbers of women in developing countries obtain secondary (high-school) education — they marry later and have fewer, healthier children.
  • Educated women have greater economic opportunity, raising the cost of more children relative to income.
  • Family planning: access to contraception and reproductive health care is critical to enabling women's choices.

6.7 · Global Comparisons — Ecological Footprint Analysis

Who Consumes How Much?

Ecological footprint analysis estimates the area of land and water required for each category of consumption and waste disposal, enabling comparisons across individuals and countries.

  • As developing countries grow economically, their per-capita footprints rise.
  • Sustainability requires both reducing per-capita consumption in wealthy countries and enabling lower-impact development in poorer ones.

6.7 · How Is Population Connected to Consumption?

I = PAT: Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology

32×
the richest billion consume & pollute more than the other 7 billion combined
  • Devised by Holdren and Ehrlich in the 1970s: environmental impact reflects not just how many people exist, but how much each consumes and what technologies they use.
  • As fertility falls and economies grow, living standards rise — driving higher consumption per person.
  • Ecological footprint: estimates the environmental impact (land and water area required) related to each person's consumption (resource use) and waste.
  • Overshoot: when resource use exceeds Earth's ability to replenish.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
6.7 · Is the Problem Overpopulation or Rising Consumption?

Both Matter — But in Different Ways

  • When countries improve economically, rising standards of living drive higher consumption per person — even as population growth slows.
  • A large population of low-consuming people may have less environmental impact than a smaller population of high-consuming people.
  • The I = PAT framework weighs the impact of each variable.
  • Industrialization: generally corresponds to higher ecological footprint.
  • Sustainability: meeting current needs without compromising future generations.

6.8 · What Can I Do?

Population, Consumption, and Your Choices

  • Know your personal ecological footprint at footprintcalculator.org, then make targeted changes.
  • Get involved with education programs that help young people in developing countries stay in school.
  • Advocate for women's education, health care access, and economic opportunity. These are the most effective approaches to voluntary population stabilization.
  • Understand the difference between population size and consumption. Reducing your footprint matters regardless of where you live.
