What Influences Birth and Death Rates?

Understanding the forces behind each rate explains why different societies take different paths — and why the transition takes the shape it does.

Factors That Lower Birth Rates

  • 📚Women's EducationSecondary schooling is the single strongest predictor of fertility decline. Educated women marry later and have fewer, healthier children.[14]
  • 🏙️UrbanizationCity life raises the cost of raising children and reduces the agricultural need for large family labor.[10,13]
  • 💊Access to ContraceptionVoluntary family planning enables women to choose the number and spacing of children.[13]
  • 📉Falling Infant MortalityAs more children survive, families need fewer births to achieve a desired family size.[10]
  • 💼Women's Workforce ParticipationEconomic opportunity raises the opportunity cost of having many children, especially for mothers.[13]
  • 📺Media & Cultural NormsMedia portraying smaller families normalizes lower fertility even before full economic development.[11]

Factors That Lower Death Rates

  • 💉Vaccines & AntibioticsImmunization campaigns and antibiotic treatment eliminate many historically lethal infectious diseases.[13]
  • 🚰Clean Water & SanitationSafe drinking water and sewage systems dramatically reduce cholera, typhoid, and diarrheal death.[13]
  • 🌾Food SecurityAgricultural improvements and distribution networks eliminate most famine-related mortality in industrialized societies.[13]
  • 🏥Healthcare InfrastructureHospitals, trained physicians, and emergency care reduce mortality from injuries, childbirth, and chronic disease.[10]
  • ☮️Reduced Conflict & ViolencePolitical stability and reduced warfare lower premature mortality, particularly among young men.[13]
  • 📈Rising Living StandardsHigher incomes correlate with better nutrition, housing, and access to preventive care — all reducing mortality.[10]
Stage-by-Stage: What's Driving the Rates?[9,13]
Stage Why Death Rates Are Where They Are Why Birth Rates Are Where They Are
Stage 1
Pre-Industrial		 Disease, famine, and war keep death rates high and volatile. No vaccines, antibiotics, or modern sanitation. Large families are essential — many children die before adulthood. Cultural and agricultural economies strongly incentivize many births.
Stage 2
Mortality Transition		 Vaccines, antibiotics, DDT (for malaria), clean water, and better nutrition sharply reduce death rates. Infant mortality falls first. Family norms haven't changed yet. Women still average 5–7 children. Cultural expectations, limited contraception, and rural economies sustain high birth rates.
Stage 3
Fertility Transition		 Death rates remain low and stable as medical infrastructure is now embedded in society. Urbanization, girls' education, contraceptive access, and falling infant mortality all reduce incentive and ability to have large families. Norms begin to shift.
Stage 4
Low Stationary		 Death rates are low but slowly rising as populations age — more elderly people with higher natural mortality. Birth rates stabilize near replacement (TFR ≈ 2.1). Women have strong economic incentives to limit family size. Contraception is widely available.
Stage 5
Declining		 Death rates tick upward as the aging population means a larger share of people at end-of-life ages. Below-replacement TFR. High cost of living, women prioritizing careers, and changing social norms drive fertility below 2.1. Some countries are below 1.0.
